At a Glance

Equilibra is a hybrid AMM with an adaptive price curve and an anchor that drifts toward the live market via auto-repeg, financed by a capped share of fee-funded LP-unit-value growth.

Positioning

Equilibra is a single-pool design that shifts between a stable-pair regime and a volatile-pair regime depending on its own state, without governance, migrations, or new liquidity ranges.

Stable near the anchor, robust at the edges

When the pool is balanced and trades are routine, the effective curve is flat and slippage is near-zero. As the pool drifts away from balance, the curve smoothly transitions toward a constant-product tail — slippage rises automatically, protecting the remaining inventory and keeping every swap feasible with no liquidity wall.

An anchor that learns the market

The "fair price" the pool quotes isn't pinned at deploy. A protected EMA oracle observes every swap, and an auto-repeg routine inches the anchor toward the EMA in tiny capped steps, financed entirely out of accumulated LP-unit-value growth (fee-funded — there is no separate fee bucket). LPs keep at least (BPS − repegShareBps − protocolFeePercent · 100) / (BPS − protocolFeePercent · 100) of that growth by construction; with the bundled presets (protocolFeePercent = 0) this reduces to (BPS − repegShareBps) / BPS.

Two immutable knobs, one live signal

The shape of the curve is set at deploy by two independent parameters: a (depth at the anchor) and λ (plateau width), and reshaped on every swap by a single live number — the pool's own off-balance distance D. The whole design fits in the formulas on the next page.

One Curve, One Anchor, Two Tunable Concentration Knobs

Imagine the pool as a spring anchored to the market price. Trades near the anchor flex the spring at almost no cost — the pool quotes a near-anchor price with tight slippage. The further the pool gets pulled from balance, the softer that spring becomes, so additional depletion costs the trader more and more until the curve approaches a constant-product asymptote and every remaining swap stays feasible.

Mechanically there are just four moving parts: reserves (x, y) in the anchor's units, an anchor price (priceScale) that drifts slowly toward the market via auto-repeg, and two concentration knobs — a sets how deep the central plateau is, λ sets how wide it is before the constant-product tail takes over. Everything else — quote, slope, fee, repeg gate — falls out of those numbers.

Anchor — "where I think fair is"

The pool always quotes this exact price when it's perfectly balanced. As the pool drifts off balance, the quote drifts away from the anchor in a controlled way.

a & λ — "depth and width, decoupled"

a alone sets the depth of the plateau at the anchor: A(D = 0) = a. Larger a deepens the centre. λ alone sets how wide the plateau is before the curve tilts toward constant-product: A = a / 2 at λ · D = W. Larger λ narrows the plateau; smaller λ widens it. The two effects are independent — V1 coupled them through a single α; V1.5 splits them.

Fees pay for the anchor's drift

When the market moves, the anchor doesn't jump — it creeps toward the market price, paid for out of the pool's accumulated LP-unit-value growth (which swap fees feed). By construction LPs keep at least (BPS − repegShareBps − protocolFeePercent · 100) / (BPS − protocolFeePercent · 100) of that growth — 50 % at the canonical repegShareBps = 5 000 with protocolFeePercent = 0.

How a Price Is Formed

Two formulas tell the whole story: the invariant the pool never breaks, and the slope of that invariant which becomes the quoted price.

1. The invariant — Equilibra's law of conservation

K(x, y; L) = A · L · (x + y) / 2 + (W − A) · x · y

A = a · W / (W + λ · D)   ·   D = (y − x)² / (x · y)   ·   W = WAD = 10¹⁸

Two regimes, one smooth curve

At balance (D = 0): A = a, so the linear head dominates and the curve is its deepest plateau. At deep imbalance (D → ∞): A → 0, so the head vanishes and K → W · x · y — the constant-product asymptote. The transition is smooth (a rational function of D), so there are no segments, no governance levers, and no router cliffs.

The depth scale L is recovered, not stored

L is the balance-equivalent depth of the level set passing through (x, y). The pool recovers it analytically from the pre-state via a closed-form quadratic and freezes it for the whole swap leg, so the trader and the solver agree on a single K = const level set. The forward and reverse legs of the same swap therefore identify the same L, which is what makes the kernel path-additive.

a — depth at the anchor

a is the value of A at the anchor: A(D = 0) = a. The linear head A · L · (x + y) / 2 scales with a, so larger a deepens the central plateau without changing how quickly the curve transitions to the CP tail. Production envelope [0.1 · W, 0.99 · W]; a = W is forbidden (forces the L-quadratic ill-conditioned).

λ — width of the plateau

λ sets the half-amplification distance: A = a / 2 exactly at λ · D = W. Larger λ ⇒ the amplification halves at a smaller D ⇒ the plateau narrows and the CP tail kicks in sooner. Smaller λ widens the plateau, pushing the cliff further out. Decoupled from a — the V1 single-α kernel coupled both effects; V1.5 separates them.

D — the imbalance signal

D = (y − x)² / (x · y) is a symmetric state distance between the two reserves: zero at balance, growing as one side outweighs the other. It is purely a function of state (no oracle reads, no priors), which lets the same invariant be evaluated forward and backward without drift.

Math-space coords — V1-style asymmetric lift

Before the kernel sees the reserves, the pool lifts them via a one-sided coordinate change: xMath = xWad (base axis, identity) and yMath = yWad · W / priceScale (quote axis, normalised to base units). A repeg shifts yMath only, which is what gives the auto-repeg's solvency gate a real impermanent-loss signal — the symmetric V1.5 lift used briefly during development collapsed that signal and is gone.

Why this matters in practice

On a balanced pool D = 0 ⇒ A = a and the invariant evaluates on its deepest plateau — a swap pays near-zero slippage. The instant a trade starts pushing the pool off balance, D climbs, A falls toward zero, and the invariant smoothly slides toward K = W · x · y. The curve softens, the slope steepens, and slippage on the next wei rises automatically. No governance, no router logic — one closed-form expression evaluated per swap.

2. The slope — that's your price

marginalPrice = ∂K/∂xMath ÷ ∂K/∂yMath

The price an infinitesimal swap would pay is the slope of the level set at the current point in math-space. Multiplying by priceScale converts it back into real units (e.g. USDT per WETH). At balance (xMath = yMath) this slope is exactly 1, so the live spot equals the anchor. As yMath grows relative to xMath the slope rises; as xMath grows relative to yMath it falls. That single ratio drives every downstream gate: EMA update, dynamic-fee distance, repeg activation.

What the kernel actually does on a swap

  1. Lift (x, y) into math-space, compute D, evaluate A = a · W / (W + λ · D), and recover the depth scale L from the closed-form quadratic W·L² − A·L·S − (W − A)·N = 0 with S = (x + y) / 2, N = x · y.
  2. Lock K = A · L · (x + y) / 2 + (W − A) · x · y for this swap.
  3. Solve for the post-swap (x', y') via a short secant iteration that converges in a handful of steps from a constant-product seed. Bounded gas.
  4. Charge the smoothstep dynamic fee based on how far the post-swap state lands from the anchor.
  5. Update the geometric EMA from the pre-swap marginal price (the swap's own resulting price is sampled by the next swap, once time advances) and, if the gate allows, advance the anchor by one damped log-domain step (retrying at halved magnitudes when the solvency gate refuses the full move).

Auto-Repeg & EMA

The anchor isn't fixed forever — Equilibra has a built-in mechanism that nudges it toward the real market price, but slowly and only when the pool can afford to.

Step 1 — Geometric EMA tracks the market

On every swap the pool feeds the pre-swap marginal price into a geometric (log-domain) exponential moving average: ema′ = ema · exp(ln(spot / ema) · (1 − α)). Averaging in the log domain makes the oracle exactly reciprocal-invariant — reading the same market through either token order produces mirror EMAs, where an arithmetic average would drift apart by ≈ σ²/2 of the price variance (Jensen's inequality) and subsidise one repeg direction. The EMA's half-life is configurable via emaPeriod; a large value smooths short-term volatility, a small value reacts faster to rapid moves.

Step 2 — Anchor follows EMA in tiny steps

If the geometric deviation between EMA and anchor — |max(EMA, anchor) / min(EMA, anchor) − 1| — reaches the active direction's dead-band (repegThresholdToken1UpWad while the EMA sits above the anchor, else repegThresholdToken1DownWad), the pool moves the anchor by the log-domain step anchor · exp(±min(repegStepWad, deviation / 5)), clamped to the target. When the LP-value gate below refuses the move, a halving ladder retries the same step at ÷2, ÷4 and ÷8 within the same block and commits the first affordable rung. One repeg per block maximum, a single call never overshoots the target, and the damped step converges without ringing. The geometric metric makes a ±2× move read 1.0 WAD in both directions, and splitting the dead-band per side turns direction bias into an explicit calibration knob (the bundled presets chase base-asset rallies with the tighter band).

Step 3 — Pay from fees, not from LP capital

A repeg has a real cost: the pool's reserves end up slightly off-balance versus the new anchor. Equilibra never pays for that cost out of LP principal. Instead, every swap accumulates an lpValueGrowthWad counter; the repeg gate may consume at most repegShareBps / (BPS − protocolFeePercent · 100) of that counter (the share is stored pre-scaled at initialize, so the protocol slice is funded from the LPs' residual and repeg cadence is independent of protocolFeePercent), with the remainder permanently committed to LPs.

Step 4 — Manipulation defences

Two layers stop a single trade from pumping the anchor: a symmetric spot cap into the EMA ([anchor / 2, anchor × 2]) bounds how far one trade can drag the oracle, and the block-and-second cadence guard prevents multi-step manipulation in the same block.

50 / 50 rule, in one sentence

At the canonical repegShareBps = 5 000 (with protocolFeePercent = 0, as in the bundled presets), no more than half of accumulated LP-value growth may ever be spent on re-centering the anchor — the other half is locked to LPs by the gate's own threshold formula.

Dynamic Fee

Equilibra charges a swap fee that depends on where in the curve the trade lands. Routine trades near the anchor pay the floor; aggressive trades that drag the pool further from balance pay the full base fee.

Floor near the centre

When the post-swap state stays near the anchor, the fee is feeFloorBps. LPs absorb easy flow at a friendly rate.

Smooth ramp to the base fee

As the trade pushes the pool further out, the fee climbs via a smoothstep m(r) = 2r − r² over a state distance of feeRampBps / 10 000 WAD until it reaches baseFee. The rate is resolved and charged at WAD precision (1 bps = 1e14), so a bigger trade never buys a smaller output beyond fixed-point dust.

Disabling the ramp

Set feeRampBps = 0 and the pool charges a flat baseFee for every swap. A live ramp cannot be arbitrarily narrow: deploys must satisfy feeRampBps · (10 000 − baseFee)² ≥ 12 · 10 000 · (baseFee − feeFloorBps)² (FeeRampTooNarrow otherwise) — on a steeper ramp the fee on the whole notional would grow faster than the input itself.

Simulator Disclaimer

What the simulator can and cannot tell you

The simulator runs Equilibra alongside two reference AMMs (constant-product and a concentrated YieldBasis AMM) on the same trade schedule, oracle path and arbitrageur model so that the relative behaviour of the AMMs can be compared under identical conditions.

Results for every AMM — Equilibra included — may diverge from what the same code would do in production. The benchmark replays historical price data against deterministic actors (single arbitrageur model, fixed gas estimates, no MEV competition, no real-world latency, no validator ordering games, no oracle outages). Real markets are richer and noisier than any replay can capture.

On top of that, gas accounting, fee precision, slippage policy and edge-case behaviour of every AMM modelled here are simplifications of their on-chain counterparts. Numbers you see are good enough to surface trends — fee capture, IL profile, repeg cadence, drawdowns under the same price path — but they are not a literal forecast of any specific production deployment.

Treat the dashboard as a lab bench: useful for comparing designs, sizing parameter ranges, and pressure-testing ideas. Treat any single dollar number as a directional estimate, not a guarantee.

FAQ

What's the difference between the anchor and the EMA?

The anchor is the curve's centre — the price the pool quotes when both sides are perfectly balanced. The EMA is a smoothed reading of where the pool's swaps have actually been pricing things lately. Repegs slowly move the anchor toward the EMA, never the other way around.

Why doesn't x · y = k hold here?

Equilibra's invariant is K = A · L · (x + y) / 2 + (W − A) · x · y with A = a · W / (W + λ · D). At the anchor (D = 0) A = a and the linear head dominates — a deep plateau scaled by a. At deep imbalance (D → ∞) A → 0 and the invariant collapses to K = W · x · y, so every swap remains feasible without a liquidity wall.

What do a and λ actually do?

The two knobs are decoupled: (1) a sets plateau depth — A(D = 0) = a, so larger a deepens the centre without moving the cliff; and (2) λ sets plateau width — A = a / 2 exactly at λ · D = W, so larger λ narrows the plateau (cliff closer to the anchor) and smaller λ widens it. The V1 single-α kernel coupled these two effects; V1.5 splits them so presets can dial depth and width independently.

Can I disable the dynamic fee or the auto-repeg?

Yes. Set feeRampBps = 0 for a flat baseFee and repegShareBps = 0 to shut the auto-repeg gate permanently. The fee triple (baseFee, feeRampBps, feeFloorBps) stays adjustable later through the pool creator's parameter timelock (every change waits a full queue window and is re-validated against the deploy-time invariants). repegShareBps = 0 is different: it is immutable in both directions — a pool deployed with the gate shut can never re-open it, and a live gate can never be set to zero.

The full runtime-adjustable set (pool-creator admin, via the parameter timelock): the fee triple (baseFee, feeRampBps, feeFloorBps), repegStepWad (at most 2× per queued change), both repeg dead-bands, repegShareBps (user-facing floor 5 000; the stored, protocol-fee grossed-up share is capped at 9 500, so the user-facing ceiling shrinks with protocolFeePercent — e.g. 7 125 at 25 %), and parachuteBandMult. Every change waits a queue delay (24 h public / 10 min private pools), stays executable for a 7-day grace window, and is re-validated against the deploy-time invariants at execution. Immutable forever: a, λ, emaPeriod, protocolFeePercent. LPs who disagree with a queued change have the whole delay window to exit — removeLiquidity is never gated.

Does an LP ever lose money to a repeg?

By construction the gate may consume at most repegShareBps / (10 000 − protocolFeePercent · 100) of the pool's accumulated LP-unit-value growth (the share is stored pre-scaled at initialize). The remainder of the growth is locked to LPs as part of the threshold formula and cannot be reclaimed by the gate. With the canonical 50 %/50 % setting and protocolFeePercent = 0, LPs are guaranteed to retain at least half of the accumulated LP-unit-value growth that swap fees feed — there is no separate fee bucket.

Where do I see the live oracle state?

Two getters, both on the pool: getOracleState() returns the storage snapshot (last value written by a swap), and getLiveEmaPrice() returns the EMA decayed up to block.timestamp on the fly.
